Suppose a task finishes after several hidden waiting stages. Only the total time is visible, and different members of the population have different stage counts and speeds. Can repeated observations reveal the hidden population and predict what happens when every stage speeds up?

An exact observation threshold

Suppose each individual has at most mmm independent exponential stages, where the bound m≥2m\ge2m≥2 is known. In a matched group, the runs are independent conditional on the individual’s stage count and rates, which stay fixed across runs. The population joint distribution of mmm matched totals uniquely determines the population distribution of stage counts and unordered rates.

With m−1m-1m−1 runs per individual, two populations can have exactly the same distribution of observations. After the same positive increase in every stage rate, they can give different probabilities of finishing by any given positive deadline.

For a population with at most two stages, this means measuring each individual twice and keeping the pairing. Even the exact distribution of unpaired totals can leave the response to a rate increase undetermined.

Estimating the response directly

The intervention adds a known rate θ\thetaθ to each stage. An explicit unbiased statistic uses matched totals to estimate the resulting completion probability, with a uniform guarantee when the hidden rates have a positive lower bound.

Connection to existing work

Vandermeulen and Scott study mixture identification from grouped observations, with thresholds controlled by the number of mixture components. Wei and Nguyen study identification and estimation from repeated observations of latent types.

Here the controlling quantity is the number of exponential stages within each type. The population may mix arbitrarily many types, the number of stages can be unknown, and repeated rates are allowed. The proof uses the special transform structure of exponential sums to obtain the stage-count threshold.

Related result: what a checkpoint changes

A separate discrete-time model uses independent geometric stages and multiplies their success probabilities by a known factor. Its exact total-time distribution identifies the accelerated law. An intermediate checkpoint can also reduce the observation cost: in explicit examples, one checkpoint removes the growth in episode count caused by a long known block of stages.
